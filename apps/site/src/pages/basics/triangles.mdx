## Everything is triangles

<Diagram name="triangles" height={270} rows={0} caption="The computer cuts a star into triangles, one after the other." />

The GPU, the chip that draws, knows one shape: the triangle. A triangle is three corners, its vertices. The GPU fills millions of triangles a second. The computer cuts every other shape into triangles first. A rectangle is 2 triangles, the star above is 10, and a smooth curve is many.

More triangles cost more: the runtime has to place each vertex in every frame in which it moves. So a runtime wants as few vertices as the picture allows.

## A picture on the triangles

<Diagram name="texture-sample" height={280} rows={0} caption="A triangle on the screen, and the picture it takes its colors from. The square is a pixel inside the triangle, and the circle is where it reads the picture." />

The pictures of a game are textures: images the GPU can read. Each vertex of a triangle names a place on the texture, its texture coordinate, written u and v from 0 to 1. For each pixel inside the triangle, the GPU averages the three places. The closer the pixel is to a vertex, the more that vertex counts. Then the GPU reads the texture at the average place.

So the picture follows the triangle. Move a vertex, and the part of the picture near it moves with it and stretches. [Vertices](/how/vertices/) shows that on a real mesh, with the seam where two triangles meet.

## What this means for animation

Every attachment of a skeleton is triangles with texture coordinates on an atlas page: a rectangle for a region, many triangles for a mesh. Animating a skeleton means moving those vertices in every frame. [The GPU and draw calls](/basics/gpu/) shows how they reach the GPU.
