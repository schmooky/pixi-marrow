## Everything is triangles

<Diagram name="triangles" height={270} rows={0} caption="A star is cut into triangles, one after the other." />

The GPU, the chip that draws, knows one shape: the triangle. A triangle is three corners, its vertices, and the GPU fills millions of them a second. Every other shape is cut into triangles first. A rectangle is 2 triangles, the star above is 10, and a smooth curve is many.

More triangles cost more: each vertex has to be placed in every frame it moves. So a runtime wants as few vertices as the picture allows.

## A picture on the triangles

<Diagram name="texture-sample" height={280} rows={0} caption="A triangle on the screen, and the picture it takes its colors from. The square is a pixel inside the triangle, and the circle is where it reads the picture." />

The pictures of a game are textures: images the GPU can read. Each vertex of a triangle names a place on the texture, its texture coordinate, written u and v from 0 to 1. For each pixel inside the triangle, the GPU mixes the three places by how close the pixel is to each vertex, and reads the texture at the mixed place.

So the picture follows the triangle. Move a vertex, and the part of the picture near it moves along and stretches. [Vertices](/how/vertices/) shows that on a real mesh, with the seam where two triangles meet.

## What this means for animation

Every attachment of a skeleton is triangles with texture coordinates on an atlas page: a rectangle for a region, many triangles for a mesh. Animating a skeleton means moving those vertices in every frame. [The GPU and draw calls](/basics/gpu/) shows how they get to the GPU.
