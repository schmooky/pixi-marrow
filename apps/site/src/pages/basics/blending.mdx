## Alpha

Each pixel of a texture has a color and an alpha: how much it covers, from 0 for invisible to 1 for solid. The pictures of a skeleton are mostly not rectangles, so the space around each shape has alpha 0.

## Blending

When the GPU paints a pixel over another, it combines the two colors. That is blending, and the rule decides the look:

<Diagram name="batches-blend" height={190} rows={2} caption="A pixel on the screen, a color painted over it at half cover, and the result. Pick how it blends." />

Normal blending covers what is under the new color by its alpha. Additive blending brightens what is under it, which is how glows and sparks are made. Multiply darkens it, like a shadow. Each rule is a setting of the GPU, and changing the setting between two pictures ends a batch, as [The GPU and draw calls](/basics/gpu/) explains.

## Overdraw

<Diagram name="overdraw" height={400} rows={1} caption="Three pictures overlap, each a round shape in a transparent rectangle. Each pixel shows how many times it was painted. Pick how they are drawn." />

The GPU paints every pixel of every triangle it draws, also where the texture is transparent and nothing changes. Drawn as whole rectangles, the three pictures paint many pixels two or three times, mostly in their invisible corners. Cut to their shapes, the counts drop, and the picture is the same. Painting a pixel more than once is overdraw. It is the main GPU cost of 2D games, and phones have little GPU time to spare.

## What this means for animation

A skeleton is many pictures painted over each other in its draw order, most of them with transparent space around them. pixi-marrow can cut those transparent parts with outlines. It also keeps additive and normal slots in one batch, as [Draw order and batches](/how/batches/) and [Why it is fast](/how/fast/) show. From here, the [guide](/start/) shows how to use the runtime, and [How it works](/how/frame/) takes a frame of it apart.
