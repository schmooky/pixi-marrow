## Local and world

<Diagram name="pose-chain" height={210} rows={3} caption="A two-bone arm. The upper bone sways. Pick whether the lower bone keeps its own rotation or turns too." />

The lower bone keeps a local rotation of 30 degrees, yet it points in a new direction in every frame, because its parent turns. Its world rotation is the world rotation of its parent plus its own local rotation. The numbers under the drawing show that sum. The dashed line shows where it would point with its local rotation alone.

The animation of the last page wrote values like "the neck turns 12 degrees". That is a local value, relative to the parent bone. To draw a wing, the runtime must know where the wing is in the skeleton after the neck, the body and the root turned it. That place is its world transform.

The world transform of a bone is the world transform of its parent, combined with the local values of the bone. Pick **neck** and watch the numbers. Its first child, the body, keeps its local rotation the whole time. Its rotation on screen changes, because its parent turns.

The two numbers count differently on purpose. A local rotation counts counter-clockwise, as in the Spine editor. The rotation on screen counts clockwise, as a Pixi `angle` does.

## One pass, parents first

In a skeleton, a parent bone always comes before its children. So one pass from the first bone to the last computes every world transform. Each bone finds its parent already done.

A world transform is six numbers: a 2 by 2 matrix for rotation, scale and shear, and a position. The pose of pixi-marrow keeps them for all bones in one typed array. The render step reads them from there with no copy.

## What follows a bone

Pick **neck**: 22 bones below it turn, nearly the whole bee. Pick **hand**: one bone below it. Pick **wing**: none. The slots of a bone and their images move with it. So, when a picture of many parts must move, the animator turns one bone.

Pick **antenna**. The antennae are part of the mesh of the head, 41 vertices weighted to 7 bones. A vertex follows several bones by weight, so the antenna bends and the face around it stays in place. The next page shows how.

A bone can also refuse part of what its parent does. A crown can stay upright while the head tilts. For that, the animator sets the bone of the crown to take the position of its parent and not its rotation. Every bone of the bee takes everything from its parent.

## Constraints

Keys are not the only thing that moves a bone. A constraint changes bones during the pass, from other bones:

- **IK** turns a chain of bones so that its end reaches a target, such as a foot on the ground.
- **Transform** copies rotation, position or scale from one bone to others.
- **Path** moves bones along a curve.
- **Physics**, from Spine 4.2, lets bones swing and settle like springs: hair, antennae, a dangling coin.
- **Slider**, from Spine 4.3, plays an animation by a value instead of by time.

The animator sets the order in which bones and constraints update. From that order, the runtime makes one list for each skin, the update cache of the official runtime. In pixi-marrow, each skin gets that list once, and every copy that wears the skin shares it.

The Bzzz symbols have no constraints. Characters and props with hair, chains or cloth often do. A physics constraint keeps moving while the skeleton rests. Also, it does not see a change of the Pixi position by itself. [Good to know](/good-to-know/) shows how to tell it.

## What pixi-marrow skips

The official runtime computes every bone in every frame. But pixi-marrow computes a bone again only when its local values, its parent or the place of the skeleton changed. The line under the preview counts them. When the neck turns, the runtime poses 23 bones again in each frame. When a wing turns, it poses one.

So the cost of a frame follows what moves, not the size of the skeleton. A skeleton whose animation ended poses no bone at all. The runtime also skips a path constraint while nothing that it reads changed. Every world transform still equals the official one to the bit.
