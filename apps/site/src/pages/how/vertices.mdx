## Triangles

The GPU draws triangles and nothing else. Each corner of a triangle is a vertex. A vertex has two things: a position on the screen, and the place on the atlas page that its pixels come from. Every frame, the runtime computes those positions from the pose of the bones.

## A region

Pick **wing**. A region is a rectangle: 4 vertices and 2 triangles, and one bone moves them all. The animator placed the image next to its bone with an offset, a rotation and a scale. Those 4 corners relative to the bone never change, so the runtime computes them once.

<Snippet source={source} regions="region" />

Each frame then needs only the world transform of the bone. The runtime multiplies the 4 corners by it, and gets their positions in the skeleton.

## A mesh

<Diagram name="vertices-weights" height={230} rows={1} caption="A strip of picture along two bones that bend at the elbow. The color of each vertex shows its weights: blue for the upper bone and orange for the lower. Pick rigid or weighted." />

With **rigid** vertices each one follows a single bone, so at the elbow the strip creases like folded paper. With **weighted** vertices, the ones near the joint follow both bones, part each. The bend spreads over them like skin over an elbow. That is what a weighted mesh is for. Each weight costs one more bone to ask, for every vertex, in every frame it moves.

Pick **body** in the preview at the top. A mesh is a polygon cut into triangles. This one has 9 vertices. 8 of them follow two bones, the neck and the body. One of them follows the neck by 0.31 and the body by 0.69.

A weighted vertex keeps a position next to each of its bones. The runtime asks each bone where it would put the vertex, and mixes the answers by the weights. So when the body bends away from the neck, the vertices between them spread the bend over the mesh. The body bends and does not break.

<Snippet source={source} regions="mesh" />

Pick **head**. Its 41 vertices follow 7 bones, and 18 of them follow two. A vertex in the middle of an antenna follows `head2` and `head3` by half each. That is why the antenna curves when its bones turn. Move the pointer over the bee to pick a vertex.

## Where the pixels come from

<Diagram name="vertices-uv" height={220} rows={0} caption="A picture with a grid, drawn as two triangles. One corner moves." />

Each vertex keeps a fixed place on the picture. When a corner moves, each triangle stretches its part of the picture to its new shape. Where the two triangles meet, the grid lines bend, because the GPU maps each triangle on its own. A mesh with more triangles hides that bend.

On the right of the preview is the atlas page of the Bzzz symbols, with a box where the image lies. Under it, the preview magnifies that part of the page and draws the same triangles on its pixels.

The place of a vertex on the page never changes. An animation moves the vertex on the screen and never on the page. So the GPU stretches the pixels of each triangle to its new shape. A packer can also turn an image to fit the page better. The places on the page already include that turn.

An animation can move the vertices of a mesh directly too, with deform keys. The runtime adds those offsets before it applies the weights.

## What it costs

A region costs 4 vertices, however it moves. A mesh costs as many vertices as it has, and a weighted vertex costs work for every bone it follows. The attachments of the bee have 161 vertices in all: 21 regions of 4, and 6 meshes with 77.

The runtime batches a skeleton of up to {facts.batch.autoVertices} vertices with the skeletons around it. So the bee and the other skeletons of its set share draw calls. A larger one gets meshes of its own. In both modes the CPU places the vertices, unless a game sets `skinning: 'gpu'` for the mesh mode.

## What pixi-marrow does

The vertex math is the arithmetic of the official runtime, and it is public in `pixi-marrow/core`. The runtime computes the corners of every region once into a layout, and every instance of the asset shares it. Each frame, an instance places, colors and uploads only what changed since the frame before.

With `skinning: 'gpu'`, a skeleton in the mesh mode leaves the weighting to the vertex shader. It then uploads its bone matrices, 24 bytes a bone, in place of its vertices. The CPU still places a mesh while a deform is on it, and a region with a sequence. The setting does nothing in the batched mode. [Performance](/performance/#skinning) says when it pays.
