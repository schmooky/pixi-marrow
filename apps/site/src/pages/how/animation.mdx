## Timelines

An animation is not a film of pictures. It is a list of timelines, and each changes one value of one part. That value is the rotation of a bone, its position or scale, the color of a slot or the image it shows.

The win of the bee has 44 timelines. The sheet puts the timelines of one part on one row: 24 bones, 8 slots and a row for events. A bone that the animation does not name keeps the pose it has.

## Keys

A key is a value at a time. The dots of a row are its keys, colored by what they change. Between two keys the runtime computes the value, so a few keys make a smooth move.

Look at the rows of the wings, `wingl` and `wingr`. Each has 41 keys that alternate between two angles, one every 2 frames. That is the flapping of the wings, and it is all keys, because the animator wanted that exact rhythm.

## Curves

A key also says how the value moves to the next key. Linear moves at an even speed. Stepped holds the value and jumps at the next key. A bezier curve eases in and out, as the animator drew it in the graph editor.

In the win, 95 moves between keys are bezier, 87 stepped and 13 linear. The keys of the wings are stepped, so a wing jumps from one angle to the other and the eye sees a blur.

## Images and colors

The rows that start with `slot` change slots. A white square swaps the image a slot shows: the hands of the bee come out and the open smile takes the place of the joy face. The pink dots change the color of the closed eyes, which fade in and out.

This is how a symbol turns blurred and dims as well. Its `blur_in` swaps images and its `disable` changes colors. Pick `blur_in` to see it.

## Events

The yellow diamond at 0.33 seconds is the event `onBeeWow`. An event key changes nothing in the pose. The runtime emits it when the time of the animation passes it, and the game plays a sound there or starts a counter. [Start](/start/) shows how to listen for it.

## From a time to a pose

At every update the runtime moves the time of each track. For each timeline it finds the keys before and after that time, follows the curve between them, and writes the value into the pose of its bone or slot. A looping animation starts its time again at the end.

During a mix two animations apply at once. The outgoing one fades as the incoming one grows, over the time of the mix.

## What pixi-marrow does

pixi-marrow computes every value with the same arithmetic as the official runtime, and its poses equal the official ones to the bit. It finds keys the same way too, by a scan from the first key. A lookup reads 2.4 keys on average, and a cursor that remembered the last key was measured and did not pay.

What it skips is work that cannot change the picture:

- An animation that would apply the same values as in the update before is left out. That is an animation past its end, or one whose timelines all hold one value, such as `disable`.
- A bone is posed again only when its own values, its parent or the place of the skeleton changed. A symbol at rest so costs close to nothing.
